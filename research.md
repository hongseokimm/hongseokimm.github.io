    <h3 class="entry-title">The Intergenerational Welfare Cost of Social Security Insolvency</h3>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <details class="paper-abstract">
        <summary>Abstract</summary>
        <p>Social Security will not be able to pay full scheduled benefits after its trust fund runs out. If the government guarantees the retirement benefits, should it borrow or raise taxes? And who pays? I study this question in a general-equilibrium overlapping-generations model, calibrated to 2010 U.S. economy, in which people choose how much to save, how many hours to work, and how much to invest in skills. A guarantee has two parts. First, it removes uncertainty about future benefits. But a clear promise to pay lower benefits would do the same. Second, it requires taxpayers to pay for the funding gap. The guarantee helps people alive today, especially older people, but future generations pay for it through higher taxes and more public debt. As a result, a guarantee is a reason to borrow less, not more. It makes lower public debt more valuable. When society puts more weight on future generations, it is better to reduce debt sooner, but this is costly for people alive today.</p>
      </details>

  <li>
    <h3 class="entry-title">Generosity, Legibility, and Fertility Inertia</h3>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <details class="paper-abstract">
        <summary>Abstract</summary>
        <p>Large family-policy budgets have often produced surprisingly small changes in fertility. The programs that do move births tend to share a different feature: households can tell what they will receive and for how long. I study this distinction in a dynastic fertility model in which parents see public news and policy announcements but are unsure how closely either maps into their own future. A cautious parent evaluates each family plan using the least favorable interpretation she cannot rule out. This behavior creates fertility inertia: moderately adverse news reduces welfare without changing fertility, investment per child, or consumption. It also makes the perceived value of a transfer depend on its worst-case household-level pass-through rather than on the government's expenditure. An opaque program must therefore promise more than a transparent entitlement to generate the same fertility response. The analysis suggests that family policy has two distinct margins, generosity and legibility, and that increasing the first without improving the second can make fertility less responsive to subsequent news.</p>
