  <li>
    <h3 class="entry-title">The Intergenerational Welfare Cost of Social Security Insolvency</h3>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <details class="paper-abstract">
        <summary>Abstract</summary>
        <p>This paper asks which generations bear the welfare cost of Social Security insolvency. The trust fund is expected to be depleted in 2033, but each generation meets it at a different age, with different years of benefits at stake and different means of adjusting. In a life-cycle model with endogenous saving, labor supply, human capital, and claiming, I quantify how the expected duration of reduced payments shifts that welfare incidence. The main result is that the cohort losing the most unpaid dollars is not the cohort losing the most welfare. Households that expect the shortfall self-insure by saving, working, and human-capital investment, but the cost of doing so depends on age. The youngest cohorts spread the adjustment over a career, whereas mid-career cohorts must compress it into the years before retirement and end up worse off than if the shortfall had been a surprise. Duration matters as well. When the shortfall is brief, the burden lands mainly on retirees, and when it is prolonged, the burden shifts to the working generations behind them.</p>

  <li>
    <h3 class="entry-title">Fertility Inertia under Ambiguity Aversion</h3>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <details class="paper-abstract">
        <summary>Abstract</summary>
        <p>High family-policy spending often produces only small changes in fertility. I study this problem in a Barro–Becker model. Parents value descendants’ utility, dislike risk in its total value, and assess family plans by their worst-case value. They are uncertain about how well public news predicts children’s outcomes. Information inertia creates an interval of bad news in which welfare falls but locally optimal fertility, investment per child, and consumption remain unchanged. Reliable subsidies can still raise fertility. An explicit policy solution discounts uncertain benefits by the lowest possible delivery rate. Thus, unclear delivery requires larger promises for the same fertility response. More effective support raises fertility and shifts and widens the inertia interval. Less uncertainty about signal informativeness narrows it for a fixed family plan. This suggests two policy dimensions: generosity and legibility. The exact inertia result is limited to maxmin preferences, continuous fertility, and a local normal representation.</p>
